## Overview

BUDT 751 (Spring 2026, seven-person team). Small and mid-sized health insurers, Medicaid administrators, and third-party claims processors identify suspicious claims through manual review and static rules today. That misses novel fraud patterns, over-flags legitimate providers, and slows payment on the flagged ones. We built a decision-support system — not a replacement — that scores providers and claims and hands prioritized cases to human investigators.

## Problem

Two failure modes we designed against.

**Rule-based systems miss novel patterns.** Static rules encode fraud we've seen before. Fraudsters iterate.

**Black-box ML denials burn trust.** Insurers can't legally auto-deny claims without human review. Any system that ignores that fails deployment before it ever ships. The guardrails have to sit in the routing layer, not in a policy PDF.

## Business context

Concrete stakeholders. A fraud investigator wants a prioritized queue and a per-case reason. A payments analyst wants a threshold they can defend to a regulator. A compliance lead wants the responsible-AI story documented. The Streamlit dashboard has different pages for each, because they're different jobs.

## Data sources

- **Primary.** Kaggle _Healthcare Provider Fraud Detection Analysis_. Beneficiary demographics, inpatient claims, outpatient claims, provider-level fraud labels — merged into a single provider view.
- **Secondary.** A synthetic claim-level dataset used for Streamlit demo panels where entering realistic PHI would be inappropriate.

Provider-level target: `PotentialFraud` (binary).

## Feature engineering

Fifteen-plus features aggregated to the provider level. Individual claims are noisy; provider aggregates catch the systemic patterns fraudsters emit across many claims.

- Total claims count
- Inpatient / outpatient claim counts
- Average and total reimbursed claim amount
- Average and total deductible amount
- Unique beneficiaries per provider
- Unique physicians per provider
- Average claim duration
- Average chronic-condition count per beneficiary
- Average patient age
- Percentage of claims with an admitted diagnosis code
- Unique diagnosis and procedure codes

## Technical choices, and why each one

**Provider-level modeling over claim-level.** Claim noise wrecks recall. Provider aggregates surface the coordinated patterns that individual claims can hide.

**Three-model bake-off with sklearn fallback.** XGBoost isn't always installable on constrained environments. Graceful degradation to sklearn's boosting matters if this ever runs on an insurer's actual infrastructure.

**Threshold policy in code, not in a doc.** Three business thresholds (0.30, 0.50, 0.70) are configurable in the Streamlit UI. Compliance leads can tune them without a code deploy.

**Human-review floors.** Any claim over $10,000, any prediction under 80% confidence, and any provider without prior fraud history all bypass auto-denial paths. These are enforced in the routing layer, not left to a policy document nobody reads.

**Streamlit for the delivery layer.** Insurance analysts aren't going to install Jupyter. Streamlit gets them a real interface for the cost of not building a frontend. If the product ever ships, that layer gets rebuilt in React; the model plumbing behind it stays.

## Evaluation

Class imbalance in fraud detection is severe. Accuracy alone would be misleading. We reported accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC, confusion matrix, and top-flagged precision at operational thresholds.

The primary provider-level Logistic Regression hit ROC-AUC 0.951. Random Forest and XGBoost were within a few points, and used for robustness rather than as the primary decision maker.

## Responsible AI

Non-negotiable for a healthcare case study. The difference between "I trained a model" and "I built a system a hospital could deploy" sits inside this section.

**False positive cost.** Denied legit claims delay care and damage provider trust.

**False negative cost.** Paid fraudulent claims eventually raise premiums for everyone.

**Guardrails.** Ten-thousand-dollar floor, 80% confidence floor, no-history no-auto-denial. Enforced in the routing layer.

**Explainability roadmap.** SHAP for per-provider explanations is in the future work list; investigators need to see what drove a flag, not just the flag itself.

**Limitations acknowledged.** Kaggle labels reflect potential fraud, not final legal determinations. The synthetic secondary dataset doesn't capture real operational complexity. Real deployment would need drift monitoring, audit logging, and a policy owner.

## Lessons learned

**The delivery layer is half the project.** A dashboard a claims analyst can actually use is worth more than a marginally better AUC that never ships.

**Guardrails belong in code.** Any policy that lives only in a document eventually gets ignored under time pressure.

## Future improvements

- SHAP-based local explanations per provider.
- Network features across providers, physicians, and beneficiaries; fraud is often a graph.
- Probability calibration and threshold optimization against investigation cost, not just against AUC.
- Analyst feedback loop feeding a retraining pipeline.
